## What I want

The story arc and screenplay for **World 01: The Big Night** — one Friday night, from knock-off through to Saturday morning, following the canon cast.

One night, one town, one crowd. Everything happens on the same Friday, in the same town, and **the town is never identified** — not by a real name and not by an invented one. It is the viewer's own town, wherever that is. No named city, no skyline, no landmark, no bridge or stadium or harbour, no signage or plates legible enough to place it.

Unnamed is not the same as unspecific. Build a real place: the same pub, the same servo on the same corner, the same takeaway, the same stretch of side street between them, consistent enough that a returning viewer recognises it without ever being told what it's called. Unmistakably Australian and located nowhere in particular — that combination is the point and it is harder than either half alone. Record the places you establish; later worlds return to them.

Keep the geography walkable. Venues, food, the servo and wherever the kick-ons happen are all in reach of each other, because a group on foot at 2am is most of the texture of the night.

This is not a film that happens to get cut up for social. It is social-media content that adds up to a film. The audience meets this on Instagram and TikTok, ten to fifteen seconds at a time, in any order, often with the sound off, usually on a Friday when they're deciding whether to go out. The arc has to survive that. Then it has to reward the person who follows all of it, and pull them to the website for the full-length photography and video.

So write to two jobs at once:

- **The feed job.** Every scene must work as a standalone post. Someone who has never heard of shirtfaced sees one 12-second clip and either stops scrolling or doesn't. A scene that only makes sense as chapter seven of something is a dead post.
- **The arc job.** Across the night the scenes have to accumulate into one event with real cause and effect. Use the six phases the production system requires — setup, commitment, escalation, complication, peak, aftermath (`README.md` §2 Level 2, `STORY_ENGINE.md` §4). Someone who watches all of it should get something the single-post viewer didn't.

The night does not need a conventional plot. It needs a change in state over time, and an ending worth publishing rather than a stop.

And build the hooks. Name explicitly, per scene, what pulls someone off the feed and onto the site: the extended cut, the photo set, the frame that only exists in the gallery, the garment that's only fully legible there. Don't write "drives traffic." Write which asset lives where and why anyone would go looking for it. If a scene has no credible website extension, say so rather than inventing one.

## This is the first of a series

More worlds are coming. Different scenarios, largely the same crowd. `UNIVERSE_PREMISE.md` is explicit that World 01 is one world and not the brand, and `CHARACTER_CONTINUITY.md` treats characters as durable production entities that persist across campaigns rather than descriptions rewritten each time. Write this night as episode one of something, not as a self-contained short.

Practically that means three things.

**Establish, don't exhaust.** This night introduces the crowd to people who have never met them. Everyone in it should be legible by behaviour by the end — who instigates, who's dry, who's the chaos, who's on best behaviour. But don't spend every character trait, every relationship and every good idea in one night. A scenario that leaves nothing for World 02 has failed even if it's excellent on its own.

**Leave the cast where the next world can pick them up.** No relationship gets resolved that later worlds need open. Nobody arrives at a permanent new state. The change across the night is real, but it's the change one night makes, not the change a season makes.

**Plant what can recur.** Devices, phrases, running bits, objects, an absence that gets noticed — the things a returning viewer recognises in World 02 and a new viewer doesn't need. Kevin is available as exactly this kind of device and stays a device. Note anything you plant deliberately so it's on the record rather than rediscovered later.

The aftermath phase carries this weight. It should close the night without closing the world.

**We Lost Gary is part of this night.** The owner has settled it — see `SERIES_CONTINUITY.md` §2.1. The campaign in `06-we-lost-gary-campaign-plan.md` is not a separate later activation; it occupies this same Friday-night window and the disappearance belongs in this arc. Build five-becomes-four into the complication and peak. Nobody in the copy explains the absence. The audience notices before the brand says anything, and the brand may never say it. The mechanic only lands once, so spend it properly here rather than scattering it.

## Two clocks, not one

Keep these separate and label both everywhere they appear.

**Event time** is the story: Friday knock-off through Saturday morning.

**Publication time** is the loop: ten primary posts per platform across a 14-day cycle, roughly five feed posts a week.

The whole night does not ship on the night. A small number of assets earn real-time Friday or Saturday-morning publication because the timestamp is part of the joke; the rest are released across the fortnight. Decide which is which and say why.

## The constraints that don't move

These come from the repo. I'm restating the ones most likely to get quietly broken:

- **Nobody is named in a generation prompt.** Ever. The prompt says "one bloke," "a woman," "another mate." The screenplay may and should use the canon names — that's what `CHARACTERS.md` exists for — but anything downstream that a model will read is anonymous. Bridge the two with a stable production identity per character as `CHARACTER_CONTINUITY.md` requires: fixed appearance, wardrobe state, relationships, carried objects. "One bloke" in scene two and "one bloke" in scene nine must be resolvable to the same person without a name ever entering the prompt.
- **Supporting cast can be new, and usually stays unnamed.** The night is full of people who aren't in `CHARACTERS.md` — the bloke behind the bar, the woman who ends up in the kick-ons, the two mates already at the table when the group arrives. Write them freely and give them behaviour, but identify them by role and action rather than by name: "the barman," "someone's workmate," "the woman with the esky." A supporting figure only earns a name if the story genuinely cannot work without one, and if that happens, flag it to me as a proposed addition to canon rather than quietly writing it in.
- **Use the smallest ensemble that makes the story work.** Do not deploy all fifteen canon characters to prove you read the document. Density comes from background strangers and ordinary clutter, never from a bigger named cast.
- **Lucas and Tommy stay open.** Section 4 of `CHARACTERS.md` leaves their ages, occupations and relationship to Emma undecided. Do not decide them. Kevin is a held device, not a character you may introduce.
- **The camera observes from outside.** Not in the lift, not in the car cabin, not inside the container the subjects are in. A kitchen shot from the dining room is an observer. Read the Photography Language section and take it literally.
- **Subjects stay out of small enclosures too.** Next to, in front of, sitting on. The exception is interaction at an opening. Take the bigger version of every location.
- **Nobody enters or leaves a vehicle.** The scene happens beside the car. If a beat needs someone to arrive or leave by car, that beat is already broken — rewrite the beat, don't stage the car.
- **The tone is optimism and momentum, never intoxication as the joke.** The closed bottle shop was rejected for reading as resignation. Standing still with no next action reads as the end of the night.
- **The night has a clock, and people wear the hours.** By 4am they've been up all night and should look it. Tired but happy, never eight hours rested.
- **The Success Test applies to every scene.** Would someone share this without the product in it? If no, the scene fails, and I'd rather you cut it than defend it.

## Where the repo contradicts itself

Three places. Resolve them this way and don't silently pick a side.

**Branding.** `WORLD.md`'s Global Production Rule and the Current Canon Notes take precedence over the older blanket line in the Prompt Construction Protocol that says all packaging is blank. The current rule: anything we sell or may sell in that category is blank wherever it appears in frame — t-shirts, crops, hoodies, jumpers, hats, and the overshirts, jackets and totes the rule also names. No background exemption. Anything we don't sell may carry its real branding as incidental background clutter — never centred, never held up, never legible enough that the eye goes to it first. Its absence is what makes a photograph look staged.

**Production state.** `SHOTLIST.md` is the authority on shot-level status. Treat Rejected Drift entries in `CONTINUITY.md` as rejected *generation attempts*, not rejected scenes, unless the shot list marks the scene itself rejected. W01-015 is Planned in the shot list and appears in Rejected Drift; the sunrise balcony is still live, and its lessons stand (no phantom anatomy, sunrise subjects must visibly have been awake all night).

## The locked reference prompt

Take its form, not its furniture. `WORLD.md` says this explicitly and it is the single most common way this goes wrong. Use the sentence shape, the action-then-consequence construction, the bare-word mood block and the closing emotional line. Do not inherit its seven-person cast, its mid-twenties-to-mid-thirties age band, its kebab shop, its weather, its hour or its wardrobe allocation. All of that belongs to W01-002 and nothing else.

## Directing language

The world stays constant. The filmmaking grammar changes. That's the point of difference — one Big Night should be able to produce a tense dialogue scene, a panic-handheld sequence, a static surveillance gag, a glossy garment hero, a camcorder aftermath and a quiet dawn piece, and all six read as unmistakably shirtfaced because the structure, cast, humour and world don't move.

So assign a directing language to every post in the cycle, and don't let one dominate. `STORY_ENGINE.md` §8 is the contract for how this gets stored: presentation class, camera behaviour, pacing, performance style, lighting register, sound register, edit register.

Two rules on how you write it.

**Describe the technique, not the director.** A director's name is shorthand between us, not an instruction to a model. Write "locked symmetrical frame, centred subjects, flat deadpan performance, no camera movement" rather than naming a filmmaker — and never anything that reproduces an identifiable protected scene, character, dialogue or composition. The repo already says this and it is also the only version that survives contact with a generation model, which cannot do "in the style of" without drifting into pastiche.

**The grammar serves the scene, not the reverse.** Run the COSTUME TEST on every assignment. If the scene is only interesting because of the directing language, the treatment is supplying the personality and the scene is dead. Pick the grammar that the moment already wanted.

Useful families to draw from, technique-first: static symmetrical deadpan; snap-zoom character introductions; hyper-cut mundane inserts treated dramatically; controlled dark precision framing; dreamy observational youth drift; raw flash-lit party documentary; absurd premise played completely straight; handheld anxiety and overlapping pursuit; naturalistic real-location street work; fisheye and heroic low-angle glorification of the ordinary; jump-cut broken continuity with direct address; mockumentary interview and reaction; static surveillance wide with timestamp; serious-reportage language applied to a trivial subject; long-lens nature-documentary observation; slow minimal luxury-fashion composition; MiniDV and camcorder texture with blown highlights and digital zoom; lo-fi available-light handheld. There are more — horror, exploitation, infomercial, procedural, reality TV, sports broadcast, dashcam, video diary. Reach for one that fits rather than one that's novel.

Map each assignment to its presentation class so the cycle audit works: most of these fall into documentary or rough, a few into polished, and the formally strange ones into weird.

## What to hand me

**One — The Arc.** Two pages at most. What happens across the night, what changes from the first frame to the last, who it happens to, and where the tension and the joke live. The human truth underneath the whole thing, stated once, plainly. If the arc needs a paragraph of explanation to land, it's the wrong arc. Then run it against the nine story quality gates in `STORY_ENGINE.md` §5 and tell me the results in a line each.

**Two — Cast and wardrobe.** Which of the canon cast are in this night and which aren't, and why. Any new supporting figures, listed by role, with what each is there to do, marked clearly as new rather than mixed in with the canon list. Then the production identity table that bridges names to anonymous prompts:

| Screenplay name | Production identity | Fixed appearance | Starting wardrobe | Relationship behaviour |

Garment allocation per person, with continuity changes across the night — the jumper that gets borrowed, the hoodie that ends up tied around a waist, the shoes that come off. Hero product rotation across scenes, checked against the rotation state in `CONTINUITY.md`.

**Three — The Screenplay.** Scene by scene, in event order. Each scene gets:

- Scene ID and status (approved / planned / new / connective), event timestamp, location, lighting state
- Who's in it, by production identity, and what they're doing *with each other* — not each busy alone
- The action, written as action, not as description of a mood
- The legible social event and its consequence. This can be a quiet exchange, a discovery, a decision, a handoff, an absence — it does not have to be the whole group reacting to one thing. Ten scenes of everyone laughing at one bloke is one scene ten times.
- What happened five seconds before and what happens five seconds after
- Hero product, product prominence level (P0–P3 per `WORLD.md`), and camera position — all checked against what's already been used
- Continuity in and out: wardrobe, carried objects, fatigue, weather, ground condition
- The social cut: what the 10–15 second post is, and what the caption does or deliberately doesn't say
- The website hook: which longer-form asset this scene feeds, at what prominence, and whether it's an alternate frame, a longer cut or a distinct extraction

Write the scenes tight. Short declaratives. Present tense. The Form section of `WORLD.md` governs the voice of any prose that will become a prompt.

**Four — The posting sequence.** Map the scenes onto the ten-post loop in `README.md` §2, across the 14-day cycle, against the Friday rhythm. For each of the ten slots give:

- Event timestamp and publication day
- Loop role (hero film, photo story, micro film, character film, found film, closer)
- The Instagram execution and the TikTok execution *separately*. Instagram runs 7 motion / 3 photographic; TikTok runs 8 motion / 2 photographic. That mismatch means one Instagram photo story needs a genuinely TikTok-native conversion — camera-roll reconstruction, photo-motion edit, spoken account, slideshow with native pacing — not the same export uploaded twice. Don't convert every photo set into fake video either; the cycle depends on real format contrast.
- Directing language, written as technique rather than as a director's name, and its presentation class
- Presentation language (polished / documentary / rough / weird), audited at the end against the §4 target mix of roughly 30 / 40 / 20 / 10. Rough describes the authored format, not broken anatomy or unusable media.
- Why that asset belongs in that slot

Stories sit outside the ten. Add a Story reservoir per scene where there's something worth having — alternate cuts, ambient fragments, detail shots, outtakes — and leave it empty where there isn't. There's no quota to fill.

**Five — What carries forward.** Short. Written to the schema in `studio/worlds/SERIES_CONTINUITY.md` §1 so it can be lifted straight into that register: cast state on exit, relationships as left, planted devices, locations now canon, what was held back and why, what was spent. This is the handover note to the next world, not a summary of this one. Flag anything you hit that belongs in that document's open-decisions section rather than solving it.

**Six — Discarded candidates.** Two or three scenes you considered and cut before the final draft, with the specific reason: weak human truth, repeated emotional beat, continuity problem, product-led construction, couldn't stand alone, or a YEAH, I GUESS reaction. These don't appear in the screenplay or the posting plan. I'd rather have eight scenes that are FUCK YES than fourteen that are technically compliant.

## How to work

Do the work. Don't narrate what you're about to do, don't ask me to confirm the approach first, don't hand me an outline and wait. If a decision is genuinely mine — something the canon leaves open and the story can't proceed without — make the call, mark it clearly as a call you made, and keep going.

Before you show me anything, run it yourself:

- Does every scene pass the Success Test without the product?
- Has any scene put the camera or a subject inside an enclosure? Does a P2 or P3 extraction sneak one in?
- Does anyone get in or out of a car? Does anyone lean through a door opening? Is a seated passenger squared to the door instead of the windscreen?
- Does every operated control have the thing it operates in frame?
- Is any garment we sell carrying a mark? Is any unsold brand centred or legible first?
- Does the hero product rotate, and does the cap face front somewhere without every cap wearer turning to camera?
- Does each character keep one production identity across the night?
- Do fatigue, hair, make-up, wardrobe layers, carried objects, weather and ground condition all progress continuously?
- Are Lucas and Tommy still open? Is Kevin still a device?
- Could anyone name the town from any frame? A landmark, a skyline, a plate, a piece of signage, an invented place name? If so, it's broken.
- Is the town nonetheless a specific consistent place rather than generic mush?
- Does the disappearance land, and does anything anywhere explain it? Nothing should.
- Does the ending close the night without closing the world? Has anything been spent that World 02 needed?
- Is every character legible by behaviour to someone meeting them for the first time?
- Does each photographic carousel carry 4–8 frames that earn their sequence, rather than filler?
- Does any directing assignment survive the COSTUME TEST, or is the treatment supplying the personality? Is any director named where a technique should be described?
- Is the humour delivered straight, or is it arriving in a clown suit?
- Did you explain something that was funnier unexplained?
- Is any scene there only to bridge the arc? Cut it and make the arc shorter.
- Is the reaction FUCK YES, or YEAH, I GUESS?

YEAH, I GUESS is a rejection.

Write it to a new file at `studio/worlds/world-01/STORY_ARC.md`. Do not edit `WORLD.md`, `CONTINUITY.md`, `SHOTLIST.md` or any other canon or production-state file. Where those files conflict or lack state you need, record the problem in your new document rather than resolving it silently.

Then tell me, in one paragraph, the single thing about this arc you're least sure of.
