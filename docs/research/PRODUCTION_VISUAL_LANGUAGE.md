# SHIRTFACED — Production Visual Language

## Status
Active — constitutional research pass 05

## Purpose
Define the physical graphic-production mechanics that distinguish premium apparel from cheap merchandise. This document concerns how artwork is built for fabric, ink, embroidery and ageing. It does not define concepts, humour, stories or campaign direction.

## Evidence Basis
This pass synthesises the already documented cross-brand corpus in:
- `GRAPHIC_DESIGN_SYSTEM_RESEARCH.md`
- `GRAPHIC_ARCHETYPE_TAXONOMY.md`
- `GRAPHIC_COMPOSITION_MECHANICS.md`
- `APPAREL_EXECUTION_PLAYBOOK_FINDINGS.md`

Repeated evidence already established that premium brands commonly specify screen printing, garment weight, fit, wash and specialty applications together. Examples in the existing corpus include conventional screen print, water-based ink, puff, flock, crack, high-density, brushed-screen, rhinestone, embroidery, mineral wash, stone wash, sunbleach and vintage wash.

This pass converts that evidence into production rules. Exact mesh counts, minimum line widths and ink-film thicknesses remain printer-specific and must be confirmed through production testing.

# Constitutional Findings

## Finding 01 — Production Technique Is a Modifier, Not the Idea

### Observation
Premium brands use specialty applications selectively. The underlying graphic remains legible before puff, flock, high-density, crack, rhinestone or wash effects are applied.

### Mechanism
A weak composition does not become premium because the ink is thicker or shinier. Specialty production changes tactility, reflectivity, depth or ageing behaviour; it cannot create hierarchy or meaning by itself.

### Constitutional rule
Every design must pass a flat one-colour proof before any specialty production modifier is approved.

The proof must retain:
- dominant silhouette;
- focal point;
- hierarchy;
- readable typography;
- recognisable brand or graphic identity.

If the design fails without its effect, the effect is rescuing the artwork and must be removed.

### Confidence
High.

---

## Finding 02 — Screen-Print-First Construction Is the Core Standard

### Observation
Screen print is the most consistently named production method across the existing premium-brand corpus.

### Mechanism
Screen printing rewards decisive shapes, controlled colour separation, strong edges and deliberate tonal systems. These constraints naturally reinforce graphic clarity.

### Constitutional rule
Unless another method is structurally essential, artwork must be constructed so it can be reproduced as separated spot-colour screens.

Source artwork must provide:
- discrete colour separations;
- transparent background;
- no accidental raster fringe;
- no hidden blend modes;
- no unsupported live effects;
- explicit underbase requirements;
- named garment colour;
- named ink colours or approved equivalents.

### Confidence
High.

---

## Finding 03 — Every Graphic Requires a Line-Weight Hierarchy

### Observation
Strong apparel graphics rely on forms that survive fabric texture, ink spread, wash and normal viewing distance. Ultra-fine detail is repeatedly identified as a production risk.

### Mechanism
Equal fine line weight across every element produces visual mush and inconsistent printing. A hierarchy of structural, secondary and detail lines preserves form when the smallest information softens.

### Constitutional rule
Illustrated artwork must define three line roles:

- **L1 — Structural:** outer silhouette, major separations and primary shapes. Must survive reduction, ink gain and garment movement.
- **L2 — Secondary:** internal structure, facial or object definition and important texture boundaries.
- **L3 — Detail:** optional surface information. The design must remain complete if some L3 information is lost.

L3 detail must never carry recognition, anatomy, essential text or the joke.

Exact minimum widths are production thresholds, not constitutional constants. They must be set by the printer, mesh, ink and final print size.

### Confidence
High for hierarchy; variable for numeric thresholds.

---

## Finding 04 — Tonal Systems Must Be Deliberate

### Observation
Premium graphics use solid spot colours, controlled halftones or deliberately processed photographic separations. Cheap results often come from unresolved full-colour imagery pushed directly onto fabric.

### Mechanism
A deliberate tonal strategy determines which information remains solid, which becomes halftone and which disappears. It also controls contrast and ageing.

### Constitutional rule
Every design must choose one primary tonal system:

1. **Flat spot system** — solid separated colours with no tonal simulation.
2. **Spot plus halftone system** — solid anchors with controlled halftone tone breaks.
3. **Reduced photographic system** — image converted into a deliberate limited-tone separation.
4. **Process or simulated-process system** — permitted only when full-colour reproduction is essential and tested on the final blank.

Mixing tonal systems without a reason is prohibited.

### Confidence
High.

---

## Finding 05 — Halftones Must Describe Tone, Not Add Decoration

### Observation
Halftones are useful where photographic or illustrated tone is required, but indiscriminate dot texture creates visual noise and an artificial “vintage” appearance.

### Mechanism
Halftones work when they translate value transitions into printable information. They fail when applied as a blanket texture independent of light, material or form.

### Constitutional rule
Halftones may be used for:
- shadow transitions;
- photographic value reduction;
- controlled colour mixing;
- atmospheric depth;
- selected tonal modelling.

Halftones must not be used merely to make clean artwork look old.

Each halftone field must have:
- a tonal purpose;
- a defined screen angle and frequency set by production;
- sufficient dot size to survive printing and washing;
- no isolated highlight dots that will disappear unpredictably.

### Confidence
High.

---

## Finding 06 — Distress Is a System, Not a Filter

### Observation
Distress and wash effects recur in premium apparel, but they are normally tied to a specific visual language, garment wash or reproduction reference.

### Mechanism
Uniform digital grunge applied over everything flattens hierarchy and signals imitation. Controlled distress can imply age, abrasion, ink loss, photocopying, weathering or material wear, but each produces different marks.

### Constitutional rule
A distressed design must select one primary distress logic:

- **Ink abrasion:** loss concentrated on raised or exposed printed areas.
- **Cracked film:** directional breaks consistent with a thicker ink layer flexing.
- **Faded deposit:** reduced density rather than random missing chunks.
- **Photocopy degradation:** threshold noise, edge breakup and generation loss.
- **Weathered surface:** directional erosion linked to environmental exposure.
- **Garment interaction:** print and wash designed to age together.

Generic all-over grunge overlays are prohibited.

Distress must preserve:
- main silhouette;
- key facial or object features;
- counters in letters;
- critical punctuation;
- small permanent identity marks.

### Confidence
High.

---

## Finding 07 — Texture Must Follow Material or Reproduction Logic

### Observation
Premium graphics can be highly textured, but the strongest examples appear internally coherent rather than assembled from unrelated texture packs.

### Mechanism
Texture feels intentional when it explains a surface, print process, source image or ageing condition. Unrelated textures create inconsistency and obscure the focal point.

### Constitutional rule
Every texture must answer one question:
- What material is this?
- What reproduction process produced this?
- What physical event aged this?
- What lighting or tonal function does this serve?

If none applies, remove the texture.

No design may use more than two unrelated texture families without a documented compositional reason.

### Confidence
Medium-high.

---

## Finding 08 — Edge Treatment Controls Perceived Quality

### Observation
Graphic edges can be crisp, distressed, soft through halftone, mechanically outlined or intentionally misregistered. Problems arise when edge character changes accidentally within one composition.

### Mechanism
The edge is where artwork meets garment. Inconsistent edge logic makes separate elements appear sourced from unrelated systems.

### Constitutional rule
Every design must declare a primary edge language:

- crisp vector edge;
- hand-inked irregular edge;
- halftone-softened edge;
- distressed or eroded edge;
- photographic threshold edge;
- intentional registration offset.

Secondary edge treatments may appear only where they perform a clear hierarchy or material function.

Unintentional anti-aliased halos, white mattes and raster fringes are production failures.

### Confidence
High.

---

## Finding 09 — Registration Error Must Be Designed or Eliminated

### Observation
Slight misregistration can support vintage, punk, photocopied or hand-produced aesthetics. Random production drift on otherwise precise artwork simply looks defective.

### Mechanism
Intentional registration offset creates rhythm and energy when direction, colour and amount are controlled. Accidental offset harms detail and legibility.

### Constitutional rule
Registration treatment has two allowed states:

1. **Controlled registration:** separations are aligned and traps/chokes are specified for reliable production.
2. **Designed misregistration:** offset is included in the artwork, visible in proofing and consistent with the selected visual language.

“Let the printer misregister it naturally” is prohibited as a design instruction.

### Confidence
High.

---

## Finding 10 — Colour Count Must Be Earned

### Observation
Premium brands use both restrained and complex colour systems. The consistent mechanic is not a universal maximum; it is purposeful colour assignment.

### Mechanism
Every additional ink introduces another visual voice, screen, registration relationship and cost. Unnecessary colours dilute hierarchy.

### Constitutional rule
Every ink must perform at least one role:
- dominant field;
- subject separation;
- tonal depth;
- focal accent;
- permanent identity;
- required photographic information.

Colours that perform the same role must be consolidated unless their difference is essential.

The one-colour version must be reviewed even when the production design uses multiple inks.

No universal ink-count ceiling is constitutionalised. Each design must justify its count.

### Confidence
High.

---

## Finding 11 — Contrast Must Be Evaluated on the Actual Garment Colour

### Observation
The same separation behaves differently on black, white, washed, coloured and heathered garments. Underbase, ink opacity and garment dye alter perceived colour and edge clarity.

### Mechanism
Artwork approved against a neutral digital background can fail when the garment becomes an active colour in the composition.

### Constitutional rule
The garment colour must be treated as either:
- active negative space;
- a designed colour within the artwork;
- a surface to be isolated by an underbase.

Approval requires proofs on the named garment colour. A transparent PNG preview alone is insufficient.

For dark garments, the specification must state whether the underbase is:
- full;
- selective;
- intentionally omitted;
- reduced to preserve a soft hand or vintage result.

### Confidence
High.

---

## Finding 12 — Wash and Print Must Be Designed Together

### Observation
The existing corpus repeatedly pairs graphics with mineral wash, stone wash, sunbleach, vintage wash and aged finishes.

### Mechanism
Garment wash changes background contrast, surface texture and the apparent age of the print. A pristine high-opacity print on a heavily aged blank may look disconnected; an intentionally softened print can integrate with it.

### Constitutional rule
Wash is part of the colour and contrast specification, not a blank-selection footnote.

Each design must state:
- blank wash state;
- whether the print should appear new, softened or aged;
- expected contrast after wash;
- whether garment-to-garment variation is acceptable;
- whether the print is applied before or after the relevant treatment where production allows variation.

Artificial distress must not duplicate or fight the natural garment wash.

### Confidence
High.

---

## Finding 13 — Print Hand Is Part of the Visual Language

### Observation
Water-based, discharge-like soft prints, conventional plastisol-style deposits, puff, flock and high-density applications produce visibly different relationships with fabric.

### Mechanism
The print can sink into the garment, sit on top, rise above it, feel velvety or create a hard dimensional surface. This changes both appearance and wearer perception.

### Constitutional rule
Every design must declare its intended print-hand category:

- **Embedded:** soft, fabric-integrated appearance.
- **Surface:** opaque deposit clearly sitting above the garment.
- **Dimensional:** puff or high-density structure.
- **Textile:** flock, chenille, appliqué or embroidery-led surface.
- **Reflective/special optical:** appearance changes with lighting or angle.

The selected hand must support the design’s hierarchy. It must not be chosen solely because it is available.

### Confidence
High.

---

## Finding 14 — Specialty Effects Require Containment

### Observation
Specialty methods are strongest when assigned to a focal element, logo, line or defined region rather than spread indiscriminately across complex art.

### Mechanism
Containment creates contrast between ordinary and special surfaces. Applying the same effect everywhere removes the hierarchy the effect was meant to create.

### Constitutional rule
Puff, high-density, flock, foil, reflective, silicone, rhinestone and similar treatments must be assigned to a named component:
- identity mark;
- headline;
- outline;
- focal symbol;
- single texture field;
- selected highlight.

A specialty application may cover the full artwork only when the entire graphic is conceptually dependent on that physical treatment.

### Confidence
Medium-high.

---

## Finding 15 — Embroidery Requires Redrawing, Not Conversion

### Observation
Caps, chest marks and premium garment details commonly use embroidery, but complex print art does not transfer directly.

### Mechanism
Stitch direction, density, pull, small counters and fabric structure alter shapes. Automatic conversion preserves paths, not visual intent.

### Constitutional rule
Embroidery artwork must be purpose-built with:
- simplified silhouettes;
- enlarged counters;
- reinforced small gaps;
- limited internal detail;
- defined stitch direction;
- approved fill and outline logic;
- test sew-out on the actual fabric or cap structure.

Print files must not be sent directly for embroidery digitisation without a simplification review.

### Confidence
High.

---

## Finding 16 — Portability Requires Controlled Variants

### Observation
The same visual identity may appear across tees, hoodies, caps, tanks and crops, but direct size reduction or enlargement causes production and composition failures.

### Mechanism
Each garment has different available zones, seams, pockets, drape and production methods.

### Constitutional rule
A graphic family may have controlled variants:
- full composition;
- simplified emblem;
- one-colour version;
- embroidery version;
- chest identifier;
- sleeve or neck signature.

Variants must preserve the same dominant silhouette or identity relationship. They are not automatic exports from one master file.

### Confidence
High.

# Production Technique Classes

## P1 — Core Screen Print
Flat spot-colour separations. Default production language.

## P2 — Soft-Hand Screen Print
Water-based or comparable embedded result where fabric integration is part of the desired appearance.

## P3 — Reduced Photographic Print
Limited-tone photographic or thresholded art constructed specifically for screen reproduction.

## P4 — Dimensional Print
Puff or high-density application assigned to a controlled element.

## P5 — Textile Surface
Embroidery, flock, chenille or appliqué used as the primary surface language.

## P6 — Optical Modifier
Reflective, metallic, foil, glow or other light-dependent effect used selectively.

## P7 — Hybrid Construction
Two or more materially different methods with explicit hierarchy and compatibility testing.

Each product must nominate one primary class. Additional classes are secondary modifiers.

# Permanent Production Rules

1. Artwork must pass one-colour proofing.
2. Artwork must have an intentional line-weight hierarchy.
3. Every colour must perform a defined role.
4. Every texture and distress treatment must have a physical or reproduction logic.
5. Garment colour and wash must be included in composition approval.
6. Specialty effects must be assigned to named components.
7. Registration error must be either controlled or deliberately designed.
8. Source files must contain production-ready separations.
9. Garment-specific variants must be redrawn where necessary.
10. Final approval requires physical sampling or a printer-approved equivalent.

---

# Prohibited Production Shortcuts

- Generic grunge texture over the entire design.
- Automatic image tracing used as final line art without manual cleanup.
- Full-colour raster art submitted without separation strategy.
- Unchecked thin strokes, tiny counters or isolated halftone dots.
- Raster artwork enlarged beyond its effective resolution.
- White matte or halo around transparent artwork.
- Relying on accidental printer misregistration for character.
- Adding puff, flock or foil to make weak typography interesting.
- Sending print artwork directly to embroidery without redrawing.
- Approving colour only on a backlit screen.
- Assuming one file can be printed unchanged on every garment colour.
- Applying identical distress to image, typography and permanent identity regardless of hierarchy.

# Production-First Design Checklist

Before artwork approval:

- [ ] Primary production class selected.
- [ ] Canonical garment, colour, weight, fit and wash named.
- [ ] One-colour proof remains recognisable.
- [ ] Dominant silhouette survives reduction.
- [ ] Structural lines are materially stronger than detail lines.
- [ ] Small text and letter counters are production-safe.
- [ ] Colour count is justified role by role.
- [ ] Halftones have tonal purpose.
- [ ] Distress follows one defined physical logic.
- [ ] Texture families are coherent.
- [ ] Edge language is consistent.
- [ ] Underbase strategy is named.
- [ ] Registration strategy is named.
- [ ] Specialty effects are contained and assigned.
- [ ] Print hand supports the intended visual result.
- [ ] Garment wash and print ageing do not conflict.
- [ ] Front, back, sleeves, hood, pocket and seams have been checked.
- [ ] Production variant files are separated from presentation mockups.

# Print QA Checklist

## Pre-production
- Verify art size against the approved garment block.
- Confirm separations, ink names and garment colour.
- Confirm print location from seams, collar, pocket and centre line.
- Check trapping, choking and underbase.
- Confirm halftone suitability with the printer.
- Approve a digital separation proof and, where possible, strike-off.

## Physical sample
- Inspect from distance, conversational range and close range.
- Check centring by eye on the worn garment, not only by ruler.
- Check opacity and unwanted garment show-through.
- Check small counters, fine lines and halftone retention.
- Check print hand, stretch and cracking.
- Check registration at high-contrast edges.
- Check pocket, hood and seam interference.
- Wash-test for shrinkage, colour shift, adhesion and intended ageing.
- Photograph the worn sample under ordinary light before approval.

# POD Compatibility

Print-on-demand may be used for prototypes or selected products, but the design system must not quietly degrade to suit the lowest-common-denominator process.

## Suitable for POD
- bold flat artwork;
- controlled raster imagery at correct resolution;
- strong transparent edges;
- limited fine detail;
- designs where exact spot-colour matching is not essential.

## Higher risk in POD
- subtle dark-on-dark tonal separations;
- very fine halftones;
- exact Pantone-critical colours;
- distressed microtype;
- specialty hand or dimensional effects;
- large dense prints where breathability matters;
- designs requiring precise front/back registration relationships.

## Rule
A POD version is a production variant. It must be proofed independently and may require simplification. It must not become the master artwork by default.

---

# Premium Versus Cheap-Merch Diagnostic

A premium production result usually shows:
- clear separation between structural and decorative detail;
- colour restraint or justified complexity;
- intentional edge character;
- garment-aware placement;
- coherent print hand and wash;
- physical sampling;
- specialty effects used sparingly;
- artwork that remains strong without production gimmicks.

A cheap-merch result commonly shows:
- generic full-colour art dropped onto a blank;
- no relationship between design and garment surface;
- uncontrolled tiny detail;
- random distress;
- weak contrast;
- visual effects chosen from software menus rather than physical logic;
- one master file indiscriminately resized across products;
- no worn-body or wash testing.

---

# Working Thresholds — Not Constitutional Constants

The following must be established with the selected printer and blank:
- minimum positive line width;
- minimum reversed line width;
- minimum text size;
- minimum embroidery detail;
- halftone frequency and angle;
- trap and choke values;
- maximum reliable print area;
- maximum number of screens;
- underbase opacity;
- puff expansion;
- high-density layer count;
- acceptable registration tolerance.

These values belong in product-level production specifications, not in the permanent design constitution.

---

# Required Product Specification

Every production-ready graphic garment must include:

- product name and version;
- canonical blank and supplier;
- garment colour and wash;
- fit and fabric weight;
- print surfaces and placements;
- scale role by zone;
- final artwork dimensions;
- production class;
- ink or thread colours;
- underbase strategy;
- print-hand requirement;
- specialty modifiers;
- distress and texture logic;
- source-file links;
- approved proof;
- physical sample status;
- wash-test status;
- revision history.

A graphic is not production-ready until this specification exists.
