# SHIRTFACED — 90s STREET / SURF / SKATE REGISTER
Consolidated research reference for the permanent archive layer.
Derived from 90s streetwear logo analysis, Australian angle refinement, and 90s Australian skate culture investigation.
Supersedes earlier souvenir-oriented briefs. Neutral archive: supports wordmark and full illustration equally.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. 90s STREETWEAR LOGO LANGUAGE (GLOBAL)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Core visual traits
- Logos were the design. Chest and back prints often dominated the garment.
- High contrast, limited colour: heavy black on white (or reverse); primary colour blocking for sports/hip-hop crossover.
- Typography as identity:
  - Hand-scrawled / signature script (Stüssy template — raw, personal, graffiti-adjacent)
  - Heavy condensed block / Impact-style (collegiate, athletic, hip-hop: FUBU, Karl Kani, Starter)
  - Simple geometric sans (Futura Bold Italic — Supreme box logo)
  - Outline + fill and hard drop shadows common
- Simple containers: boxes, ovals, circles, shields, rectangles. Rarely ornate.
- Motifs that travelled: 8-balls, dice, stars, lightning, arrows/chevrons, simple seals, hardware silhouettes, blank banners.
- Logo flips & parody: borrowing and subverting corporate or luxury marks was part of the DNA.
- Distress & pigment: prints that looked lived-in (cracked, faded, over-inked) already valued.

Key clusters
| Cluster              | Examples                          | Graphic signature                          |
|----------------------|-----------------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| Surf → street        | Stüssy, Quiksilver, Billabong     | Signature scripts, simple icons, bold type |
| Skate                | Supreme, Zoo York, Girl, Blind, Thrasher | Box logos, DIY/satirical, heavy type, hardware |
| Hip-hop / big logo   | FUBU, Karl Kani, Ecko, Starter, Champion | Oversized block lettering, colour blocks   |
| Sportswear crossover | Nike, Adidas, Kappa               | Bold, instantly readable marks             |

2. AUSTRALIAN ANGLE (REFINED)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

In the 90s the Australian market was dominated by the “Big Three” surf brands — Quiksilver, Billabong and Rip Curl — plus the more irreverent Mambo. These were quality, high-volume street/surf wear sold in malls, surf shops and department stores — not high fashion.

- Quiksilver: Mountain-and-wave symbol (wave over snow-capped peak, nodding to Hokusai) + clean or slightly condensed wordmarks. Strong, simple icon + type that scaled well on tees, boardshorts and outerwear.
- Billabong: Twin stylised waves inside a rectangle + bold, often slightly gothic or heavy sans lettering. Extremely readable, high-contrast marks designed for durability.
- Rip Curl: Clean, functional branding that sat comfortably alongside the other two.
- Mambo: The outlier. Loud, illustrative, often humorous or satirical graphics (Reg Mombassa and others). Less tight logo system, more bold full-print attitude.

Common language: bold, high-contrast, immediately legible marks built for real garments. Wave-derived icons, strong block or slightly stylised type, simple containers (rectangles, ovals). Distress, pigment fade and lived-in print quality were accepted and preferred.

For Shirtfaced this is the correct Australian register: quality surf/skate-adjacent streetwear energy, not souvenir illustration and not European/American luxury heraldry.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. 90s AUSTRALIAN SKATE CULTURE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Shape of the scene
Australian skate sat at the intersection of local DIY grit and imported American street/vert energy. Never as commercially dominant as the Big Three surf brands, but tightly networked, entrepreneurial, and productive of lasting infrastructure and graphics language.

Key hubs: Melbourne (Fitzroy/Prahran, Chapel Street), Sydney (northern beaches → urban street), Queensland (Gold Coast + Brisbane distribution), regional pockets (Newcastle, Canberra, Adelaide, Perth). Street overtook vert as the decade progressed.

The engine: Hardcore / Globe / the Hill brothers
Melbourne’s Peter, Stephen and Matt Hill were the central force. Former competitive skaters who started importing US product in the mid-80s (Universal Skateboard Imports → Hardcore Enterprises). Ran demos and tours (Gonzales, Hosoi, Hawk, Losi, etc.), built distribution.

- 1991: Launched Mooks with graphic designer Richard Allan — one of the first proper Australian streetwear labels with skate DNA. Name from Scorsese’s Mean Streets. Signature logo: horned lightbulb + trident. Witty, slightly dark, graphic-heavy.
- 1994: Launched Globe footwear (later apparel/hardgoods). Purpose-built for skaters, expanded into surf crossover, grew into global company (later ASX-listed, acquired Dwindle).
- Held Australian licences for Stüssy, Mossimo, Freshjive, Paul Frank and others — shaped what imported street/skate looked like on Australian shelves.

This is the quality Australian skate-to-street pipeline.

Local product & smaller players
- Cockroach Wheels (Tim Dawe, mid-80s into 90s): Australian-made urethane with cult reputation. Roach-printed treads, strong graphics, sponsored local riders. Related Cozmo efforts.
- Queensland distribution: Kwala Skateboards (Andy Mackenzie), Equal Central.
- Smaller clothing: Rad Rags (Canberra, late 80s–early 90s) — padded shorts and baggy streetwear.
- Dedicated shops relatively few but passionate (Yodgees, PSC, 99 Degrees, Daily Grind, etc.) alongside broader retailers (Surf Dive n Ski, General Pants).

Graphics & visual language
Absorbed the American wave (World Industries satire, Blind script/reaper, Girl, Alien Workshop, Thrasher flame) while developing local flavour:
- Clean or slightly distressed wordmarks and simple icons (Globe, Mooks lightbulb/trident).
- Hardware-focused marks and functional product graphics (wheels, trucks, decks).
- Humorous / irreverent edge (Mooks, Mambo crossover).
- High-contrast black/white or limited colour, bold enough for tees, stickers and board graphics.
- Less pure wildlife/souvenir, more urban, graphic, slightly dark or witty.

Key filmed history: Tic Tac 2 Heelflip (2001, Whyte House / Hill-linked) covers the full arc from 60s sidewalk surfing through 90s corporatisation and the rise of street specialists (Dollin, Mumford, Bartie, Pappas brothers).

4. WHAT THIS MEANS FOR THE SHIRTFACED ARCHIVE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Permanent layer priorities (neutral, reusable across drops):

1. Heavy block and outline wordmark treatments
2. Simple seals, plain shields, boxes, ovals (no crowns, no heraldic foliage)
3. Chevrons, arrows, star bursts, lightning, dice, blank banners
4. Skate hardware silhouettes (trucks, wheels, decks)
5. Registration / crop / print-shop marks
6. Cracked plastisol, pigment fade, photocopy, dry-screen textures
7. Clean functional icons in the Globe / Billabong / Quiksilver register
8. Slightly irreverent or witty graphic devices in the Mooks / Mambo lane (without tipping into souvenir)

Avoid
- Wildlife, native flora, national food, domestic infrastructure (souvenir register)
- High-fashion heraldry, ornate crests with crowns
- Third-party trademarks

The Hill brothers / Hardcore / Globe / Mooks story is the clearest local parallel to the “skate kids who built the infrastructure and the graphics language” pattern. That energy — entrepreneurial, graphic, slightly irreverent, built for actual clothes and boards — is the permanent-layer material.

Register summary for asset generation
Quality Australian surf/skate streetwear. 90s nostalgia. Bold, durable, high-contrast. Type-forward or device-forward. Lived-in print quality. No fashion, no souvenir.
