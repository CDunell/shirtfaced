# 1. The short version

Successful kids clothing brands do not launch with ads. They launch with a **story parents
retell**, a **collectable product architecture**, **announced scarcity drops**, an **owned
community**, and a **product-only ambassador layer** — and they add paid media later, to
scale something already working. My Mixups has an unusually good hand for this playbook:
named creatures kids choose as *theirs* is the same collector mechanic that built
Little Sleepies and Posh Peanut, without needing their fabric story. The launchpad in §7
costs almost nothing in cash; its currency is artwork, consistency, and gifted product.

# 2. What the category's winners actually did

All case evidence; sources in §9.

| Brand | Core mechanic | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Little Sleepies** (US, 2018) | Announced print drops + VIP Facebook group (~400K) + email early access + SMS "it's live" | $200M+ revenue in 2023; retired prints resell above retail |
| **Posh Peanut** (US) | Weekly drops (started as inventory management), VIP FB group, celebrity organic adoption | Collections sell out in ~5 minutes; $41M+ in 3 years after adding full-funnel paid |
| **Kyte Baby** (US) | Community-first: defined "Kyte mom" persona, IG Lives, open ambassador tier + invite-only influencer tier, fan-run FB groups | Grew almost entirely on word of mouth; limited colourways created a resale market |
| **Rylee + Cru** (US, 2014) | Illustrator-founded; original hand-drawn artwork and muted palettes in a primary-colour market | ~2,000-piece DTC runs → 800+ boutiques in 25+ countries; the illustration style is the moat |
| **Jamie Kay** (NZ) | Community-story social; shoppable UGC gallery as storefront | ~687K IG followers; global expansion |
| **Caden Lane** (US) | In-house photo studio as a daily content engine | 132% growth, ~850K followers across IG/FB/TikTok/Pinterest |
| **Bonds Baby Search** (AU) | UGC photo contest with deliberately un-precious categories ("milk drunk", "poop face") | 60K+ entries, 500K+ votes (2014); the benchmark AU baby UGC machine |
| **Goldie + Ace** (Melbourne) | One hero piece (vintage overalls) people come for first, then cross-buy | Sustained indie AU label |
| **Halcyon Nights** (Melbourne) | Artist-collab prints; distribution through museum/gallery shops (MCA, GOMA, Heide, Melbourne Museum) | Credibility channel most kids brands never touch |

**The recurring pattern across every case:**

1. A founder story parents retell (usually "parent solves own problem"; for art-led
   labels like Rylee + Cru, "the artist behind the creatures" works the same way).
2. A hero product or hero artwork, not a broad range.
3. **Scarcity drops announced in advance** — the drop calendar is the reason to follow,
   the reason to email, and the engine of urgency.
4. An **owned community** (VIP Facebook group) that gets previews and a voice in what
   gets made next.
5. A **brand-rep / ambassador layer** paid in product, generating a steady UGC pipeline.
6. Email/SMS as the conversion channel, with early access as the list-growth bribe.
7. Paid ads arrive **after** organic proof, to scale — never to launch.
8. A buy/sell/trade resale market eventually emerges and becomes free marketing and
   social proof (Little Sleepies bamboo PJs have traded at $1,000+).

# 3. Platform playbooks

## 3.1 Instagram — the home base

- **Brand-rep programs are a codified institution in kidswear**, with their own discovery
  hashtags: `#brandrepsearch`, `#brandrepkids`, `#kidbrandrep`, plus aggregator accounts.
  Published terms across brands converge on: public engaged parent account, child in the
  size range, 3+ posts/Reels per month, 3 styled photos per product within 7–14 days,
  3-month terms, compensation ladder from discount code → free product. Cash cost ≈ $0;
  cost is product + postage. (Case evidence.)
- Launch mechanics that recur: drop calendar in Stories, countdown stickers, IG Lives for
  product deep-dives (Kyte), shoppable UGC galleries (Jamie Kay).
- **Giveaways**: simple follow/tag/share works. **Avoid loop giveaways** — follower
  spikes of prize-seekers, post-contest crashes, engagement damage, and they sit against
  Instagram policy. (Platform guidance + case evidence.)
- Professional (public) account from day one — Instagram only recommends content from
  public professional accounts. (Platform guidance, already established in
  `docs/launch-research/02`.)

## 3.2 TikTok — parents, not kids

- **The audience is parents.** Parenting content pulls 120B+ views/year; #MomTok/#DadTok
  are among the most engaged communities; 72% of millennial parents say social media
  influences baby/kids purchases. (Benchmark.)
- Organic formats proven at small scale, all native to this operation: founder-led
  **packing-orders** videos (an established genre with its own discovery pages), design
  process / behind-the-scenes, character reveals, "designing this creature at the kitchen
  table". Volume and consistency beat polish.
- TikTok Shop (where available) is affiliate-driven — affiliates drive an estimated
  60–70% of GMV, apparel commissions 10–20%. Named kids-brand results: Applecrumby +42%
  monthly sales publishing 200+ videos in a month; Tiny Buds +28% ROAS via creator
  affiliate content. (Benchmark / case evidence.) Not a launch requirement for AU; note
  it for later.
- **Policy**: TikTok branded content promoting products for minors "should not speak to
  or exhort directly to minors, nor appear overly childish in an attempt to appeal to
  minors" — creator content must be parent-voiced. (Platform policy.)

## 3.3 Facebook — groups, then ads, in that order

- **Groups are the real asset.** 80% of US parents are on Facebook daily; the winning
  move is an owned VIP group (Little Sleepies ~400K members; Posh Peanut's steered
  product decisions) plus the emergent BST buy/sell/trade ecosystem that does retention
  marketing for free. Mum-to-mum trust in groups is documented in academic research.
  (Case evidence.)
- **Meta ads benchmarks** (directional, 2025): ecommerce ~$14 CPM, ~2.2% CTR, ~$38 CPA,
  ~1.9 ROAS blended; the **baby vertical is among the most efficient (~$30 CPA)**;
  apparel ~2.3:1 ROAS; life-stage targeting of parents 28–42 reported at ~3.9x on Feed.
  Warm retargeting converts 40–60% cheaper than cold. (Benchmark.)
- Standard small-account structure: Advantage+ / broad targeting carried by strong
  creative, retargeting off engaged audiences first, cold spend last.
- **The unit-economics gate (proposal, but really arithmetic):** at a $34.95 tee AOV and
  ~$30 vertical CAC, cold paid acquisition does not pencil on a single-item order. Paid
  waits until AOV is lifted (bundles, hoodie-led offers, sibling twin-packs) and/or
  repeat purchase is proven. This is also why the storefront deliberately shipped
  without Meta/TikTok attribution plumbing (`docs/curbstamps/CURB_STAMPS_SPEC.md` §2.1)
  — that stays out until paid starts.

## 3.4 Email / SMS — the conversion channel

- Drops make email legitimate: waitlist pages per drop, **subscriber early access**
  (Little Sleepies gives email an hour's head start — deliberately, as the list-growth
  engine), SMS for the "it's live" moment. Klaviyo case: +138% YoY BFCM email revenue.
  (Case evidence.)
- The site already asks for emails ("Join the curb!") — but see §6: the form is
  currently a stub. Fixing that is Phase 0, before any audience-building starts.

## 3.5 Pinterest / YouTube — later

Pinterest is a planning surface (nurseries, capsule wardrobes, gifts) with long content
half-life — cheap to seed with existing artwork, low priority. YouTube is long-form BTS
and parent-creator hauls — skip until there's a content backlog worth repurposing.

# 4. Why My Mixups fits this playbook unusually well

Proposal throughout, grounded in the cases above.

- **The collector mechanic is already the brand.** "Each one has its own shape, name and
  personality — and kids get to decide which one is theirs" (live About page). That is
  Little Sleepies' print-collecting and Kyte's colourway-collecting, but with characters
  — stronger, because a creature can carry story content indefinitely and a kid's
  favourite is an identity, not a pattern.
- **Every new creature is a drop.** The roster grows one creature at a time by design.
  Each addition is a natural announced release: tease the silhouette → reveal → release.
  A 60-creature plan is a 60-episode content calendar before a single campaign idea is
  needed.
- **The design process is the content.** Deadpan impossible hybrids invite exactly the
  participation formats that work in this category: "what two animals is this?" guessing
  games, vote-on-the-next-mixup (Little Sleepies' VIP mechanic scaled down), name-the-
  creature rounds. Rylee + Cru proved artist-led artwork is a defensible moat; My Mixups
  has that plus jokes.
- **The register matches Australia.** Bonds Baby Search won with un-precious, funny
  categories, not saccharine ones. A dry, weird creature brand ("Good people. Weird
  kids.") sits naturally in that register — the AU evidence says lean into it.
- **One honest constraint: POD has no natural scarcity.** Printify never sells out, so
  quantity-based drops would be theatre. Scarcity must be **time-based and honest**:
  release windows ("Snu's hoodie exists for two weeks, then the vault"), seasonal
  retirements, or numbered first editions — never fake "only 3 left". Retiring a
  creature's colourway periodically also creates the archive/BST dynamic legitimately.
- **Hero-first beats range-first.** Goldie + Ace's lesson: lead with one or two hero
  creatures (the ones that test best), not twelve at once. Twelve creatures on day one
  is a shop; one creature a fortnight is a story.

# 5. Rules of the road — TECHNICAL / LEGAL REQUIREMENTS

These are not proposals.

1. **Market to parents, never to children.** Voice, targeting, channels, and creator
   briefs are all adult-facing. This is both the compliant posture and, per every case
   in §2, the commercially correct one.
2. **AANA Children's Advertising Code (Dec 2023, AU)** applies to any advertising
   *directed at children*: no pester-power appeals, advertising must be recognisable as
   such to a child, restrictions on personalities endorsing to children. Parent-directed
   advertising for kids clothing is fine — keep it that way.
3. **AU under-16 social media ban (in force 10 Dec 2025)** — platforms must exclude
   under-16s; fines to A$49.5M. Changes little for a parent-facing brand; it hard-kills
   any youth-directed social idea, permanently.
4. **Meta**: under-18s can only be targeted by age and location — irrelevant if targeting
   parents, which is the rule anyway. **TikTok**: branded content for kids' products must
   be parent-voiced (§3.2).
5. **COPPA (US)**: applies to collecting data from under-13s. The store doesn't; keep it
   so. No child-directed content channels; no collecting kids' info anywhere, including
   contest entries.
6. **Kids in content**: documented parental consent for any child appearing in brand-rep
   or UGC content (rep terms include an image-rights grant — copy the Children's Place
   pattern); no identifying info (no names + schools + locations together); respect the
   growing faceless/back-of-head framing norm; never post someone's child without the
   consent chain written down.
7. **Contest/giveaway law (AU)**: keep giveaways games of skill (judged, e.g. funniest
   caption) or check state trade-promotion permit requirements before any game of chance.

# 7. The My Mixups launchpad — 90 days

Proposal. Assumes near-zero cash budget; currency is product, postage, and consistency.
Cadence assumptions are defaults to adjust against real capacity.

## Phase 1 — Foundations and quiet build (days 1–30)

- Close every Phase 0 gap. Nothing publishes until emails actually persist.
- **Decide the founder story** and write it once, in brand voice: who draws these, why
  impossible animals, why deadpan. It doesn't need to be a parent-solves-problem story —
  Rylee + Cru proves artist-origin works — but it must be retellable in two sentences.
- **Pick two hero creatures** (owner's call) to carry the launch. The other ten are the
  supporting cast, visible but not led with.
- Publish 3–4x/week across IG + TikTok (same asset, native captions): creature
  introductions one at a time, design-process material consistent with the design-system
  authority file, packing-orders once real orders exist, "what two animals?" formats.
  This is deliberately a zero-follower publishing regime — discovery-first, per the
  evidence base already established in `docs/launch-research/02`.
- Soft goal: 300–500 genuine email addresses via a single site promise — early access to
  every new creature.

## Phase 2 — Reps, community, first drop (days 31–60)

- **Brand-rep search** via `#brandrepsearch` + AU parent hashtags: recruit 8–15 AU
  parent accounts (nano is fine — 1K–10K accounts run 4–8% engagement), product-only
  terms per the codified pattern in §3.1, consent + image-rights terms per §5.6.
  Benchmark: 83% of creators work for gifting alone given brand fit.
- **Open the VIP Facebook group** ("The Curb"?) — previews, vote-on-the-next-mixup,
  name-the-creature. Small is fine; Little Sleepies' was ~20K when it steered product.
- **First announced drop**: creature #13, from the next development board. Two-week
  tease (silhouette → guessing game → reveal), email early access (24h head start),
  time-boxed release window per §4's honest-scarcity rule. Every later creature repeats
  this loop — the loop, not the creature, is the asset being built.
- One skill-based UGC giveaway in the Bonds register (funny, un-precious, judged).

## Phase 3 — Prove economics, then (maybe) paid (days 61–90)

- Run the drop loop twice more; measure list growth per drop, open→click→purchase on
  drop emails, rep-code attributable orders, and which creatures actually sell.
- Lift AOV before touching ads: sibling twin-packs, tee+cap bundles, hoodie-led drop.
- **Paid trigger (all three, else stay organic):** ≥1,000 engaged emails; one drop that
  sold >X units organically (owner sets X); AOV path to ~$60+. Then: build the
  attribution plumbing (spec §2.1), start with retargeting only at $10–20/day, and only
  then test cold Advantage+ against the proven creative. Benchmarks in §3.3 are the
  sanity check, not the target.
- 90-day review: kill what didn't move, promote what did into a standing operating
  rhythm document (the `07-friday-operating-rhythm.md` pattern, kids-label edition).

## Standing weekly rhythm (all phases)

2 creature/story posts · 1 process/BTS · 1 community/participation format · every order
packed on camera when volume allows · drop emails only when there's a drop (no filler).

# 8. The Curb Stamps launchpad, and the reusable template

## 8.1 Curb Stamps — pre-launch runway (proposal)

Curb Stamps is its own brand with its own storefront and backend already built; what's
missing is the commerce tail (POD vendor, real garment photography, live deployment —
spec §4 and §8). That ordering is normal in this category: **every case-study brand
built its audience before or alongside its store**, and waitlist-first launches are the
standard pre-launch pattern (§2, MHI pre-launch evidence in §3.3). So the unfinished
store is not a reason to wait on marketing — it defines the marketing phase:

1. **Start the audience build now, sell later.** A single waitlist landing page (the
   built site can serve it, with a working email capture) + Instagram/TikTok accounts
   in Curb Stamps' own voice. Content is character introductions — the Curb Crew's
   names and one-line personalities are already authored, 12 creatures deep, which is
   a 12-week introduction calendar without drawing anything new.
2. **Use pre-launch scarcity honestly**: "the first drop" is a real event for a store
   that has never sold anything. Founding-member framing (first 100 on the list get X)
   converts a waitlist better than a bare signup.
3. **Launch when the store is real**: POD vendor picked and wired (spec §4's to-go-live
   checklist), real garment photography replacing `GarmentArt.tsx`'s placeholder, and
   then the §7 Phase 2 drop loop applies verbatim with the waitlist as day-one demand.
4. **Distinct positioning, so the two labels don't cannibalise**: Curb Stamps is the
   bright, loud sticker-book crew ("a little universe of little guys"); My Mixups is
   the dry, deadpan hybrid label. Different registers, different grids, potentially
   different parent segments — run them as Rylee + Cru runs its sub-brands: **separate
   accounts, separate email lists, separate rep programs**, shared operating playbook
   (this document) behind the scenes. Cross-promotion is fine at the packing-slip and
   founder-story level, not at the account level.
5. Sequencing (owner's call): the shared operating cost is content cadence, and two
   simultaneous full launches means two content calendars. The low-risk default is My
   Mixups full playbook now (it can take orders), Curb Stamps in waitlist/audience
   mode until its store is finished, then a full launch with an existing list.

## 8.2 The reusable template — any future label

The template, abstracted. A new label launches by filling in nine blanks; if a blank
can't be filled, that's a brand problem, not a marketing problem.

1. **Story** — who makes this and why, retellable by a parent in two sentences.
2. **Hero** — the one or two products/characters that lead. Not the range.
3. **Collect axis** — what a kid (or parent) accumulates: creatures, prints, numbers.
4. **Drop unit** — the repeatable release (a creature, a print, a colourway) and its
   honest scarcity mechanism (time-boxed for POD; quantity only if inventory is real).
5. **Community home** — the owned group and the decision it gets a voice in.
6. **Rep layer** — product-paid ambassadors, standard terms, consent chain.
7. **List bribe** — what subscribers get that the public doesn't (early access).
8. **Paid trigger** — the numeric conditions under which ads start (never at launch).
9. **Register check** — AU evidence says funny and un-precious beats twee; §5 applies
   verbatim to every label.

One standing note for the portfolio: the Halcyon Nights museum/gallery-shop channel
(§2) is worth a dedicated look for whichever label skews more art-object.
