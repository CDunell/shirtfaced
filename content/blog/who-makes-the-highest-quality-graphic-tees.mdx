Asking "who makes the highest quality graphic tees" is really asking for a brand name, and any brand that answers with its own name is grading its own homework. More useful: the actual, checkable things that separate a good graphic tee from a bin-rack one, so you can look at any shirt — ours included — and judge it yourself.

**Fabric weight.** Anything under about 180gsm shows the shape of whatever's underneath it and goes translucent in a photo. The heavier end — 200gsm and up — holds structure, drapes properly, and survives repeated washing without turning into a rag. It's the single easiest thing to check on a spec sheet before you buy anything.

**Cotton type.** Combed cotton has the short, weak fibres removed before spinning, which means fewer of the little pills and loose threads that make a cheap tee look worn after three wears. Ring-spun goes a step further, twisting the fibres tighter for a smoother, stronger finish. Neither is marketing fluff — both are checkable, real differences in how the fabric's actually processed.

**Print method matched to the design.** Screen printing lays down thick, opaque, durable ink — the right call for a bold graphic that needs to survive fifty washes. DTG prints more like an inkjet onto fabric — better for fine detail and full-colour photographic work, worse for durability and opacity on a large single-colour design. Neither method is universally "better" — a tee using the wrong one for its own design is the actual quality problem, more often than the blank itself.

**How the ink actually behaves after twenty washes, not on day one.** Any print looks good fresh out of the packaging — that's the easiest part of quality control to fake. The real test is whether the graphic still reads clearly, still holds its colour, and hasn't started cracking along the edges after it's been through a normal wash cycle a couple of dozen times. A shop that can't show you a washed sample is asking you to trust the version of the product you'll have it least.

**Construction details that don't show up in a product photo.** Double-stitched hems that won't unravel. Shoulder-to-shoulder taping that stops the neckline stretching out after the first wash. Side seams instead of a single tube of fabric, which is what stops a tee twisting on the body over time. None of this is visible in a flat-lay photo, which is exactly why it's worth checking before you buy, not after.
