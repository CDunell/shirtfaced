Most cotton clothing gets its colour one of two ways, and the difference explains a lot about why some tees look flat and uniform straight out of the packet while others already look lived-in on day one.

Piece-dyed — sometimes called yarn-dyed or fabric-dyed depending on which stage the colour's added at — is the standard, mainstream process. The fabric (or the yarn before it's even woven) gets dyed first, then cut and sewn into a finished garment afterwards. The result is even, consistent, predictable colour, batch after batch — which is exactly why almost every mainstream tee, including most of a standard main-range blank, is made this way. Consistency is the whole point.

Garment-dyed flips the order entirely: a shirt gets constructed first — cut, sewn, fully assembled — and dyed as a finished garment afterwards. That sequence is what produces the soft, slightly irregular, already-worn-in look garment-dyed pieces are known for. Because dye takes to seams, stitching and fabric slightly differently across a finished garment, no two pieces come out perfectly identical — which is a genuine, structural reason for the variation, not a quality control failure.

That's also why a garment-dyed piece keeps changing after you own it, in a way a piece-dyed one mostly doesn't. The dyeing process leaves colour sitting closer to the surface of the fibres than piece-dyeing does, which means it keeps fading and softening gradually with every wash — on purpose, as an ongoing part of the finish, not a defect that stops once you've owned it a while. A tee like Comfort Colors' 1717, at roughly 207gsm ring-spun cotton, is a genuine example of this — the washed, faded, already-broken-in look isn't something applied once at the factory and locked in place. It's a process that keeps running in your own washing machine.

Neither approach is objectively better — they're suited to different intentions entirely. Piece-dyeing is the right call when consistency matters and you want every unit in a batch to match exactly. Garment-dyeing is the right call when the slightly worn, individual character is the actual point of the piece, and uniform colour would work against the design rather than for it.

Knowing which process a tee went through tells you what to actually expect from it over time — whether the colour you're looking at now is the colour you'll have in a year, or the starting point of something that's meant to keep evolving.
