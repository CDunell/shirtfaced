Screen printing and DTG (direct-to-garment) aren't competing versions of the same thing — they're genuinely different processes, built for different kinds of design, and the honest durability answer depends heavily on which one actually got matched to the right job.

Screen printing works by pushing thick ink through a mesh stencil, one colour at a time, straight onto the fabric. That thickness is the whole story: it sits as a real, opaque layer on top of the fibres, which is exactly why it holds colour so well on bold, simple, high-contrast designs — the kind with a small number of solid colours and clean edges. That same thickness is also what survives repeated washing so well; there's simply more ink there to wear away before the print genuinely fails.

DTG works completely differently — it's closer to a specialised inkjet printer, laying ink directly into the fibres rather than on top of them. That makes it the only sane choice for anything photographic, gradient-heavy, or using more colours than screen printing could reasonably manage without a separate expensive screen per colour. The tradeoff is real: DTG ink sits thinner and shallower in the fabric, which means less opacity — especially on dark garments, where it can look faded from day one rather than after a hundred washes — and generally less wash durability than a well-printed screen job.

So "which lasts longer" genuinely depends on the design, not just the method. A bold single-colour graphic printed via screen print will outlast the same design printed via DTG, reliably. But a detailed, multi-colour or photographic design forced into screen printing either becomes prohibitively expensive — a separate screen per colour adds up fast — or gets simplified so much it loses what made it work in the first place. In that case DTG isn't the inferior option, it's the only one that can actually reproduce the design at all.

There's a cost and flexibility side to this too, separate from durability. Screen printing needs a minimum run to make sense — someone's paying for physical screens regardless of order size, so the cost per unit only makes sense once it's spread across enough shirts. DTG has no such minimum; print one, print a hundred, same setup cost either way. That's precisely why DTG dominates the print-on-demand world and screen printing dominates real batch production — the methods sort themselves by volume almost as much as by design complexity.

Ask which method a shirt used, and for what kind of graphic, before asking which one is "better" in the abstract — it's the wrong question asked the wrong way. The right question is whether the method actually suits the specific design in front of you.
