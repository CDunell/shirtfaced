"Heavyweight" gets printed on tags constantly and defined almost never, which is a shame because the actual number behind it — grams per square metre, or GSM — is one of the few genuinely objective, checkable specs a t-shirt has. It's literally the fabric's weight measured over a fixed area, no marketing interpretation required.

Rough real-world bands, for context: anything under about 150gsm is genuinely lightweight — the kind of tee that shows the shape of whatever's underneath it and doesn't survive much abuse. 150–180gsm is the mainstream "standard tee" range most fast-fashion basics live in. 180–200gsm starts earning the word "midweight" honestly. Past 200gsm, you're in real heavyweight territory, and the two blanks most of a shirtfaced-style range gets built on — AS Colour's 220gsm main range and Comfort Colors' roughly 207gsm washed line — both sit comfortably inside that bracket.

The number matters for reasons well beyond how the fabric feels in your hand. A heavier weave holds its shape through a wash cycle instead of stretching out at the neckline and cuffs. It drapes properly rather than clinging or going see-through under normal indoor light. And critically for a graphic tee specifically, it gives a printed design a stable, opaque surface to sit on — thin fabric lets ink show through to the reverse side and distorts under stretch in a way heavier cotton simply doesn't.

There's a real tradeoff, and it's worth naming rather than pretending heavier is unconditionally better: heavyweight fabric is warmer, less breathable, and takes longer to dry after a wash. For a hot climate or a piece meant to layer easily, a lighter weight is a genuine, legitimate choice, not a downgrade. "Heavyweight" is a spec suited to a specific job — a bold, durable graphic that needs to survive years of washing — not a universal upgrade over every lighter alternative.

It's also worth knowing that GSM isn't the only fabric spec that matters, just the easiest one to compare across brands. Fibre type — combed versus regular cotton, ring-spun versus open-end — changes how the fabric feels and wears even at an identical weight. Two 200gsm tees from different mills can genuinely feel like different fabrics entirely, which is why GSM is a useful starting filter, not the whole story on its own.

One thing worth watching for: GSM measures the fabric itself, not the finished garment's construction. A heavy fabric badly cut and poorly seamed is still a badly made shirt — the number tells you about the material, not the whole product. Check both, not just the one that's easiest to put on a tag.
