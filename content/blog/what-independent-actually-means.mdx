"Independent" gets stamped on more streetwear than the word can actually support. It's supposed to mean something specific — not owned or controlled by a larger group, decisions made by the people who started it rather than a board answering to shareholders — but plenty of brands wear it as an aesthetic rather than a fact, right up until an acquisition offer proves otherwise.

The genuine version is checkable, even if most brands don't make it easy to check. Who actually owns the company. Whether it's still the founders making the calls on what gets made and what doesn't, or whether that decision now runs through a corporate parent with its own targets to hit. A brand that's been quietly absorbed into a larger group and kept its scrappy visual identity is not independent anymore — it's independent-flavoured, which is a different and much more common thing.

There's a practical tell worth checking, beyond just asking who owns the company outright: how fast decisions actually move. A design, a joke, a pricing call — at a genuinely independent operation, that goes from "someone's idea" to "actually happening" without a committee in between. Slowness isn't proof of a corporate parent, but a brand that takes months to approve something that should take a conversation is usually answering to more people than its own website admits to.

Size isn't actually the test, and it's worth saying that plainly because it's the assumption doing the most damage to this word. A small brand can be fully captured by an investor's spreadsheet. A larger one can still be entirely owner-run. Revenue and headcount tell you almost nothing about who's actually making the decisions — ownership and control are the only two things that do.

There's a real, practical upside to genuine independence, separate from the branding value of the word: nobody's approving a joke, a design direction or a pricing call against a quarterly target that has nothing to do with whether it's actually any good. That's not automatically a quality guarantee — plenty of independent work is bad on its own merits — but it does mean the reasoning behind a decision is at least the brand's own, not inherited from somewhere else's commercial calendar.

So the honest test for any label claiming it, including us: who owns it, who decides what gets made, and does the story match what's actually checkable. The ownership structure behind them is the only thing that actually does.
